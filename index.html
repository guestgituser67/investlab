Learn the Basics

Master core concepts of investing with simple definitions, practical examples, and core takeaways.

Compound Interest

Definition: Earning interest on your initial investment, and also earning interest on the interest you've already accumulated.

Example: If you invest $100 at 10% interest, you have $110 after year 1. In year 2, you earn 10% on $110, giving you $121, not just $120.

Why it matters: It is the primary engine of long-term wealth creation, allowing money to grow exponentially over time.

Index Funds

Definition: A type of mutual fund or ETF that holds all the stocks in a specific market index, aiming to match its performance rather than beat it.

Example: Instead of picking 5 tech stocks, you buy a Total Stock Market Index Fund and instantly own small pieces of thousands of companies.

Why it matters: They offer instant diversification, low fees, and historically outperform most actively managed funds.

S&P 500

Definition: A stock market index tracking the performance of 500 of the largest public companies in the United States.

Example: Leading global enterprises like Apple, Microsoft, and Amazon are heavily weighted in the S&P 500.

Why it matters: It is widely considered the primary indicator of large-cap US equities and a standard benchmark for overall market health.

Diversification

Definition: Spreading investments across various asset classes to reduce exposure to any single asset or risk.

Example: Holding domestic stocks, international equities, bonds, and cash together rather than betting on one stock.

Why it matters: It minimizes overall portfolio risk, smoothing out short-term market fluctuations.

Inflation

Definition: The persistent increase in prices and gradual decline in the purchasing power of money over time.

Example: If inflation is 3%, a basket of goods costing $100 today will cost approximately $103 next year.

Why it matters: Your money must earn a rate higher than inflation to avoid losing real purchasing power.

Stocks vs. Bonds

Definition: A stock is partial equity ownership in a firm. A bond is a debt security where you lend capital to a corporate or government entity for interest.

Example: Owning Apple stock makes you a shareholder. Owning a US Treasury bond makes you a lender to the US government.

Why it matters: Stocks offer long-term growth but higher volatility. Bonds deliver stability and regular income at lower growth rates.

Risk and Return

Definition: The fundamental rule that higher potential returns generally require accepting greater risk of price fluctuations.

Example: A high-yield savings account offers guaranteed protection with modest yield; equities experience short-term volatility for higher long-term potential.

Why it matters: Understanding risk helps investors build portfolios aligned with their risk tolerance and timeline.

Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)

Definition: Consistently investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals, regardless of market highs or lows.

Example: Automatically contributing $200 from your paycheck on the first business day of every month.

Why it matters: DCA eliminates market timing stress and ensures you purchase more fund shares when prices drop.

Expense Ratios

Definition: The annual administrative and management fee deducted by mutual funds or ETFs, expressed as a percentage of total assets.

Example: An index fund charging 0.05% costs $5 per year on a $10,000 balance, compared to $100 per year for an active fund at 1.00%.

Why it matters: Lower fees keep a significantly larger share of compound growth in your account over decades.

Cash Drag

Definition: The reduction in overall portfolio growth caused by holding uninvested cash that earns low interest compared to long-term market assets.

Example: Allocating only 60% of your portfolio to investments while leaving 40% unallocated slows down your overall compound rate.

Why it matters: Ensuring your planned capital is fully allocated avoids unintended growth slowdowns.

Lump Sum Investing

Definition: Deploying all available investment capital at once into the market immediately, rather than spreading payments over time.

Example: Investing a $12,000 bonus on Day 1 instead of contributing $1,000 each month for a year.

Why it matters: Historically, lump sum investing outperforms DCA most of the time because markets tend to rise over long periods.

Market Volatility & Panic Selling

Definition: Volatility refers to rapid price swings. Panic selling is liquidating assets into cash during a drop out of fear, locking in real losses.

Example: Selling stocks at a 35% market crash bottom and moving to cash prevents you from capturing the eventual recovery surge.

Why it matters: Staying disciplined during corrections is essential for long-term compound performance.
